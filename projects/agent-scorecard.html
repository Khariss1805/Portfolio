An agent scorecard that doesn’t reward rushing

The old team report ranked agents by handle time alone. I built a scorecard that puts speed, quality and resolution on one screen, so coaching starts from the full picture.

13 of 40agents fast but below QA target21.9%of contacts fail case notes

This is a practice project. The company is fictional and the data is synthetic, generated to behave like real contact-centre data. The questions, methods and code are the ones I would use on the job.

The question

Team leads coached on AHT because it was the number at the top of the report. Agents learned that closing contacts quickly was what counted.

The question: which agents need coaching, and on what, once speed, quality and resolution are seen together?

The data

One month of results for 40 agents: average handle time, QA score from the quality scorecard, first-contact resolution and CSAT. Plus the pass and fail result of every QA item on evaluated contacts.

What I did

  1. Wrote one SQL view per metric so every report uses the same definition, for example FCR as no repeat contact in 7 days.
  2. Modelled the data in a star schema: agents, dates and queues as dimensions, contacts and evaluations as facts.
  3. Built the dashboard below: four KPI tiles, an AHT against QA chart split by the team median and the QA target, and the QA items that fail most.
  4. Added DAX measures for targets so tiles change colour only when a target is missed.

What I found

Speed and resolution pull in opposite directions

The correlation between AHT and FCR is 0.46: agents who take longer tend to solve more at the first attempt. The ten fastest agents average 65.4% FCR, against 69.9% for the team.

Coaching should start with notes and empathy

Case notes fail on 21.9% of evaluated contacts and empathy on 17.4%. Both are easy to skip when an agent is trying to lower handle time.

What I would recommend

  • Show AHT only next to QA and FCR, never as a ranking on its own.
  • Coach the 13 agents in the fast and below-target group on notes and empathy first.
  • Ask the 9 slower, high-quality agents what they do differently, and turn it into a short guide.
