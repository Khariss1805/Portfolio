What really drives CSAT in a multilingual support desk

Every week the desk reported one CSAT number. It went up and down and nobody could say why. I broke it down by what happened in each contact.

+29.1 ptsCSAT when solved first time−14.4 ptswhen not served in own language

This is a practice project. The company is fictional and the data is synthetic, generated to behave like real contact-centre data. The questions, methods and code are the ones I would use on the job.

The question

A fictional support desk serves customers in English, Spanish, Portuguese and Dutch. Satisfaction sits around 69.5% and management wants to raise it.

The question: which parts of the customer’s experience are linked to satisfaction, and which ones can the operation actually change?

The data

18,000 contacts with a post-contact survey over six months. For each contact: reason, language queue, whether it was solved on first contact, number of transfers, waiting time, the agent’s tenure, and whether the customer was served in their own language.

What I did

  1. Checked the data first: duplicate surveys, contacts with no reason code, and waiting times that were impossible.
  2. Compared CSAT across contact reasons and language queues to see where the gaps were.
  3. For each factor, compared satisfaction with and without it, then fitted a logistic regression to check that each effect held when the others were controlled for.
  4. Turned the result into a one-page Power BI view that team leads could filter by queue.

What I found

Solving the problem the first time matters most

Customers whose issue was solved on first contact were 29.1 points more satisfied. That is more than double any other factor, including waiting time.

Cancellations and product faults pull the average down

CSAT ranges from 76% for plan changes to 60.7% for cancellations. Part of that gap comes from the reason itself, not from how the agent handled it, so these reasons need their own targets.

The Dutch queue has a language problem, not an agent problem

Only 58.1% of Dutch-speaking customers were served in Dutch. When they were, CSAT was 70.9%; when they were served in English it fell to 57.6%.

What I would recommend

  • Track FCR next to CSAT on the weekly report, and review unresolved contacts before reviewing low scores.
  • Hire or train Dutch-speaking agents for peak hours before adding agents to other queues.
  • Report CSAT by contact reason, so cancellation-heavy weeks are not read as poor service.
